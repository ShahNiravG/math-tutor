1. A hyperbola centered at the origin has asymptotes \(y=\pm 2x\) and passes through \((3,\sqrt{13})\). Find its standard equation.

Since the asymptotes are \(y=\pm 2x\), the hyperbola must be of one of the forms \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1 \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1, \] with asymptote slopes determined by \(\pm \frac ba\) or \(\pm \frac ab\).

Because the point \((3,\sqrt{13})\) has \(x^2=9\) and \(y^2=13\), we note that \(y^2>x^2\), so it is natural to expect the transverse axis to be vertical. Then the equation is \[ \frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1, \] whose asymptotes are \[ y=\pm \frac{a}{b}x. \] Given slope \(2\), we have \[ \frac ab=2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a=2b. \]

Now substitute \((3,\sqrt{13})\): \[ \frac{13}{a^2}-\frac{9}{b^2}=1. \] Using \(a^2=4b^2\), \[ \frac{13}{4b^2}-\frac{9}{b^2}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{13-36}{4b^2}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad -\frac{23}{4b^2}=1, \] impossible. So the hyperbola is not vertical.

Hence it must be \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, \] whose asymptotes are \[ y=\pm \frac ba x. \] Given slope \(2\), we get \[ \frac ba=2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad b=2a. \]

Substitute \((3,\sqrt{13})\): \[ \frac{9}{a^2}-\frac{13}{b^2}=1. \] Since \(b^2=4a^2\), \[ \frac{9}{a^2}-\frac{13}{4a^2}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{36-13}{4a^2}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{23}{4a^2}=1. \] Thus \[ a^2=\frac{23}{4},\qquad b^2=23. \]

Therefore the standard equation is \[ \boxed{\frac{x^2}{23/4}-\frac{y^2}{23}=1}. \] Equivalently, \[ \boxed{4x^2-y^2=23}. \]

2. A hyperbola centered at the origin has foci \((\pm 5,0)\). One asymptote has slope \(\frac{4}{3}\). Find the vertices.

Since the foci are \((\pm 5,0)\), the hyperbola is horizontal: \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1, \] with \[ c^2=a^2+b^2. \] Here \(c=5\), so \[ a^2+b^2=25. \]

The asymptotes are \[ y=\pm \frac ba x. \] Given one asymptote has slope \(\frac43\), we have \[ \frac ba=\frac43. \] Let \(a=3k,\ b=4k\). Then \[ a^2+b^2=9k^2+16k^2=25k^2=25, \] so \(k=1\). Hence \[ a=3,\qquad b=4. \]

The vertices are \((\pm a,0)\), so \[ \boxed{(\pm 3,0)}. \]

3. A point \(P\) lies on a hyperbola with foci \((\pm 13,0)\), and the absolute difference of its distances to the foci is \(10\). If \(P\) also lies on the line \(y=x\), find \(P\).

For a hyperbola with foci \((\pm c,0)\), the constant difference of distances equals \(2a\). We are told this difference is \(10\), so \[ 2a=10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a=5. \] Since \(c=13\), we have \[ b^2=c^2-a^2=169-25=144. \] Thus the hyperbola is \[ \frac{x^2}{25}-\frac{y^2}{144}=1. \]

Because \(P\) lies on \(y=x\), substitute \(y=x\): \[ \frac{x^2}{25}-\frac{x^2}{144}=1. \] Compute: \[ x^2\left(\frac1{25}-\frac1{144}\right)=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2\cdot \frac{144-25}{3600}=1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2\cdot \frac{119}{3600}=1. \] So \[ x^2=\frac{3600}{119}, \qquad x=\pm \frac{60}{\sqrt{119}}. \] Since \(y=x\), we get \[ P=\left(\frac{60}{\sqrt{119}},\frac{60}{\sqrt{119}}\right) \quad\text{or}\quad P=\left(-\frac{60}{\sqrt{119}},-\frac{60}{\sqrt{119}}\right). \]

Therefore, \[ \boxed{P=\left(\pm \frac{60}{\sqrt{119}},\,\pm \frac{60}{\sqrt{119}}\right)} \] with matching signs.

4. Two hyperbolas are given by

\[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1. \] If they intersect, determine the coordinates of all intersection points in terms of \(a\) and \(b\).

Let \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1 \tag{1} \] and \[ \frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{b^2}=1. \tag{2} \]

Add (1) and (2): \[ x^2\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right) + y^2\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right) =2. \] Thus \[ (x^2+y^2)\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right)=2. \tag{3} \]

Subtract (2) from (1): \[ x^2\left(\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}\right) - y^2\left(\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}\right) =0. \] Since \(\frac1{a^2}+\frac1{b^2}>0\), this gives \[ x^2-y^2=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y=\pm x. \]

So at any intersection point, \[ x^2=y^2. \] Substitute \(y^2=x^2\) into (1): \[ x^2\left(\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}\right)=1. \] Hence \[ x^2=\frac{1}{\frac1{a^2}-\frac1{b^2}} =\frac{a^2b^2}{b^2-a^2}. \] Therefore intersection points exist only if \(b^2>a^2\), i.e. \(b>a\).

Then \[ x=\pm \frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}, \qquad y=\pm x. \]

So all intersection points are \[ \boxed{\left(\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}},\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}\right), \left(\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}},-\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}\right), \left(-\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}},\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}\right), \left(-\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}},-\frac{ab}{\sqrt{b^2-a^2}}\right)} \] provided \(b>a\). If \(b\le a\), there are no real intersections.

5. A hyperbola centered at the origin has vertices \((\pm 4,0)\), and one of its asymptotes is perpendicular to the line \(3x-4y=0\). Find the equation of the hyperbola.

Since the vertices are \((\pm 4,0)\), the hyperbola is horizontal: \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1 \] with \[ a=4,\qquad a^2=16. \]

The line \(3x-4y=0\) has slope \(\frac34\). A perpendicular line has slope \[ -\frac43. \] For the hyperbola, asymptotes are \[ y=\pm \frac ba x. \] So one asymptote having slope \(-\frac43\) means \[ \frac ba=\frac43. \] With \(a=4\), this gives \[ b=\frac43\cdot 4=\frac{16}{3}, \qquad b^2=\frac{256}{9}. \]

Hence the equation is \[ \boxed{\frac{x^2}{16}-\frac{y^2}{256/9}=1}. \] Equivalently, \[ \boxed{16x^2-9y^2=256}. \]

6. Among all hyperbolas centered at the origin with asymptotes \(y=\pm x\), one passes through \((5,3)\) and another through \((13,12)\). Without graphing, decide which hyperbola is closer to its center at the vertices, and by how much.

A hyperbola centered at the origin with asymptotes \(y=\pm x\) must satisfy \(b=a\). Hence its equation is either \[ \frac{x^2}{a^2}-\frac{y^2}{a^2}=1 \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{y^2}{a^2}-\frac{x^2}{a^2}=1, \] that is, \[ x^2-y^2=a^2 \quad\text{or}\quad y^2-x^2=a^2. \]

The distance from the center to a vertex is \(a\).

Hyperbola through \((5,3)\)

Since \(5^2-3^2=25-9=16>0\), the relevant equation is \[ x^2-y^2=a^2, \] so \[ a^2=16 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a=4. \]

Hyperbola through \((13,12)\)

Similarly, \[ 13^2-12^2=(13-12)(13+12)=25, \] so \[ a^2=25 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a=5. \]

Thus the first hyperbola has vertices at distance \(4\) from the center, while the second has vertices at distance \(5\).

Therefore the hyperbola through \((5,3)\) is closer to its center, by \[ \boxed{1}. \]
