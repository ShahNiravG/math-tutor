1. Let \( t \) be a real number such that the terminal point determined by \( t \) on the unit circle has rational coordinates \( (x, y) \). If \( t \) is not a multiple of \( \frac{\pi}{2} \), show why both \( x \) and \( y \) must be of the form \( \frac{a}{c} \) and \( \frac{b}{c} \) for integers \( a, b, c \) with \( a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \). Give an example of such a point and determine the reference number for this \( t \).

Solution:

On the unit circle, the terminal point for \( t \) is \( (x, y) = (\cos t, \sin t) \) with \( x^2 + y^2 = 1 \). If both \( x \) and \( y \) are rational, then the pair \( (x, y) \) is a rational point on the unit circle.

If \( t \) is not a multiple of \( \frac{\pi}{2} \), neither coordinate is exactly \( 0 \) or \( \pm 1 \). Consider \( x = \frac{a}{c}, y = \frac{b}{c} \) with \( a, b, c \) integers, \( c>0 \), and \( \gcd(a, b, c) = 1 \). Then, \[ x^2 + y^2 = \frac{a^2 + b^2}{c^2} = 1 \implies a^2 + b^2 = c^2. \] Thus, \( (a, b, c) \) forms a Pythagorean triple. Therefore, all rational points on the unit circle arise in this way.

Example: Take the Pythagorean triple \( (3, 4, 5) \). So possible points are \[ \left( \frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5} \right) \quad \text{or} \quad \left( \frac{4}{5}, \frac{3}{5} \right) \]

The reference number for \( t \) such that \( (\cos t, \sin t) = \left( \frac{3}{5}, \frac{4}{5} \right) \) is \( t_{ref} = \arccos \left( \frac{3}{5} \right) \).

2. The terminal point of \( t \) on the unit circle is \( (x, y) \). Quickly determine, without a calculator, the terminal point for \( 5\pi - t \) and explain the symmetry involved.

Solution:

Since \( 5\pi = 2\pi \cdot 2 + \pi \), so \( 5\pi - t = \pi - t \) (modulo full revolutions the position is the same as \( \pi - t \)), and on the unit circle: \[ (\cos(\pi - t), \sin (\pi - t)) = (-\cos t, \sin t) \] Therefore, the terminal point for \( 5\pi - t \) is \( (-x, y) \).

Symmetry: This is reflection over the \( y \)-axis, as \( \pi - t \) means "mirror \( t \) across the \( y \)-axis". The same holds for \( 5\pi - t \) due to the period of \( 2\pi \).

3. Consider the sequence \( t_n = \pi + (-1)^n \frac{\pi}{6} \) for \( n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots \). For which values of \( n \) does the terminal point determined by \( t_n \) lie on the coordinate axes? Justify your answer mentally.

Solution:

Terminal points on coordinate axes have \( x = 0 \) or \( y = 0 \), i.e., \( t_n \) is a multiple of \( \frac{\pi}{2} \) (\( 0, \pi/2, \pi, 3\pi/2, \ldots \)).

Let's write out the sequence:

  • For odd \( n \), \( t_n = \pi - \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6} \).
  • For even \( n \), \( t_n = \pi + \frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{7\pi}{6} \).

Neither \( \frac{5\pi}{6} \) nor \( \frac{7\pi}{6} \) are multiples of \( \frac{\pi}{2} \). Therefore, for no value of \( n \) does the terminal point lie on the axes.

4. Estimate whether the sum \( S = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{7}\right) + \cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{7}\right) \) is positive, negative, or zero, and explain your reasoning using the symmetry of the unit circle.

Solution:

Observe that \( \cos(\theta) + \cos(2\pi - \theta) = 2\cos \theta \), so the cosines are symmetric about \( \pi \).

Notably, \( \cos(\pi - x) = -\cos x \). Let us check the numbers:

  • \( \frac{6\pi}{7} = \pi - \frac{\pi}{7} \implies \cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{7}\right) = -\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{7}\right) \)
  • \( \frac{4\pi}{7} = \pi - \frac{3\pi}{7} \implies \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{7}\right) = -\cos\left(\frac{3\pi}{7}\right) \)
  • \( \frac{2\pi}{7} \) is unique.

But the sum \( S \) is well-known in trigonometric circles:

\[ \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) = -1 \ \text{if \( n \) is odd} \]

Here, \( n=7 \), and \( k=1,2,3,\ldots,6 \), so sum is -1. If we sum over any 3 of these which are not symmetric partners, the sum is negative.

Alternatively, numerically, \( \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{7}\right) \approx 0.623 \), \( \cos\left(\frac{4\pi}{7}\right) \approx -0.222 \), \( \cos\left(\frac{6\pi}{7}\right) \approx -0.900 \).

So \( 0.623 - 0.222 - 0.900 \approx -0.499 \).

Conclusion: The sum is negative.

The reason: all three angles are in the first or second quadrant, but two of them produce negative cosines larger in magnitude than the positive one.

5. Suppose you start at \( (1, 0) \) on the unit circle and travel a total distance of \( 8\pi \) in the clockwise direction. What point do you land on? Give your answer in coordinates and explain the pattern used.

Solution:

Circumference of unit circle is \( 2\pi \). Clockwise corresponds to negative direction.

\( 8\pi \) corresponds to \( 4 \) full revolutions (\( 8\pi / 2\pi = 4 \)), so after 4 revolutions, you return to your starting point.

Therefore, you land at \( (1, 0) \).

Pattern: Every full revolution returns to \( (1,0) \), so multiples of \( 2\pi \), regardless of direction, return to the start.

6. Let \( t \) be a real number such that the terminal point on the unit circle is \( (x, y) \). If \( t \) and \( -t \) have the same reference number but different terminal points, describe fully the relationship between their coordinates, and give a nontrivial example.

Solution:

Reference number is the smallest positive angle to the \( x \)-axis. Both \( t \) and \( -t \) have same reference number, but correspond to opposite directions around the circle.

The terminal points are \( (x, y) = (\cos t, \sin t) \) and \( (\cos(-t), \sin(-t)) = (\cos t, -\sin t) \).

So, the coordinates are related by reflection over the \( x \)-axis (negating the sine).

That is:

\[ (x, y) \longleftrightarrow (x, -y) \]

Example: Take \( t = \frac{2\pi}{3} \).

  • \( (\cos t, \sin t) = (\cos \frac{2\pi}{3}, \sin \frac{2\pi}{3}) = (-\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) \)
  • \( (\cos(-t), \sin(-t)) = (\cos \frac{2\pi}{3}, -\sin \frac{2\pi}{3}) = (-\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) \)

So, \( (-\frac{1}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) \) and \( (-\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}) \) are the respective terminal points.
